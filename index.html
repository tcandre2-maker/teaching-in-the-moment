RESEARCH & EVIDENCE

Why Natural Contexts Can Matter

See how peer-reviewed studies inform naturalistic instruction, spontaneous responding, and generalization.

Research on incidental and naturalistic teaching has shown that embedding instruction within meaningful activities can support spontaneous responding and generalization. McGee, Krantz, and McClannahan (1985) found stronger generalization of preposition use following incidental teaching than traditional highly structured teaching. McGee and colleagues (1992) later showed that peer incidental teaching increased reciprocal interactions during free play. These findings support the use of natural contexts, while also reminding practitioners that generalization should be measured rather than assumed.

For RBT practice, this evidence does not mean every naturally occurring moment should become a teaching trial. The clinically relevant question is whether the moment connects to an authorized goal and whether the RBT can respond using procedures already described in the plan.

McGee, G. G., Krantz, P. J., & McClannahan, L. E. (1985). The facilitative effects of incidental teaching on preposition use by autistic children. Journal of Applied Behavior Analysis, 18(1), 17–31. · McGee, G. G., Almeida, M. C., Sulzer-Azaroff, B., & Feldman, R. S. (1992). Promoting reciprocal interactions via peer incidental teaching. Journal of Applied Behavior Analysis, 25(1), 117–126.

Peer-mediated research · 1992 · Key finding

Can peers be part of the teaching context?

McGee and colleagues trained three typically developing preschoolers as peer tutors for three young children with autism. During free play, the peers used incidental teaching around preferred toys. Reciprocal interactions increased, and adult assistance was systematically faded.

Why it matters: Important nuance: generalization was not uniform. One child increased interactions across free-play periods throughout the day; none showed increased interactions at lunch. Measure transfer rather than assuming it.

McGee, Almeida, Sulzer-Azaroff, & Feldman (1992), Journal of Applied Behavior Analysis, 25, 117–126.

The six-step sequence is a practical way to organize an RBT’s implementation when the learner’s written plan already describes the relevant target and procedure. It begins with observing what the learner does, then matching the moment to an authorized opportunity before following the plan’s timing, prompting, reinforcement, and recording directions.
